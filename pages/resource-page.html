Understanding Yourself

You may have been hearing some terms thrown around recently, as you searched for scholarships and clubs to make the most out of your college journey. You may have noticed that some of these opportunities were geared towards a very specific group of students. Rather than having a club geared towards all women, for example, it could be specifically geared towards women of color or women with autism. You may have been curious as to why an opportunity exists for such a specific subset of people if, for example, women of color could join a group dedicated to people of color and another group dedicated to women. If you asked, you may have been told that it helped to account for "intersectionality." But what is intersectionality?

Intersectionality is the idea that various social identities (race, gender, class, sexuality, disabilities, etc.) will not have the same impact on every person. Rather, these social identities inevitably interplay, affecting a person's experience of each individual social identity. It is important to consider intersectional groups, as multiple broad categories may not cover their specific subset of problems.

Another word you may have heard of is "implicit bias." This is the idea that you are unconsciously predisposed to be biased against a group of people. It is a natural phenomenon, as the human brain is designed to create categories in order to make processing more efficient and ignore extraneous information. However, when implicit biases are left unrecognized, you will be less likely or less quick to recognize situations that go against the patterns your brain has created. This can lead to awkward situations for both you and the person who was incorrectly categorized.

To help recognize implicit bias, you can take a variety of implicit bias tests (IBTs). This is a starting point for recognizing your unconscious categorizations. Keep in mind that these tests do not make any claims about their own validity, nor do they provide actionable suggestions. The tests are merely a way to get you thinking about implicit biases.
